EIA methodologies

EIA methodology refers to the structured approaches used to carry out the activities of Environmental Impact Assessment — mainly impact identification, prediction, and evaluation. A good methodology must be appropriate to the task, free from assessor’s bias, and economical in terms of cost, data, time, and manpower. The main methods used for impact identification/analysis are: ad hoc method, checklists, matrices, network method, and overlays.

Ad hoc method simplest

  • Based on subjective judgement/expert opinion; useful when there is a time constraint or lack of data.
  • Types include opinion polls, expert opinion, Delphi method.
Advantage

Specialist guidance available.

Disadvantage

Needs experts; impacts assessed on guess basis; poor identification/prediction/interpretation.

Checklists method listing

  • A simple listing of potential environmental impacts, assessing nature of impact (adverse/beneficial, short/long term, reversible/irreversible).
  • Types: simple lists, descriptive, scaling, questionnaire checklists.
Advantage

Simple to use; good for site selection and priority setting.

Disadvantage

Doesn’t distinguish direct vs indirect impact; doesn’t link action to impact.

Matrices method interaction grid

  • Provides a framework of interaction between project actions (vertical axis) and environmental impacts (horizontal axis).
Advantage

Links action to impact; good for displaying EIA results.

Disadvantage

Qualitative; risk of double-counting; hard to separate direct/indirect impacts.

Network method impact tree

  • Extends the matrix approach to capture primary and secondary impacts; builds an impact tree to show cause-effect linkages visually.
Advantage

Links action to impact; handles direct and indirect impacts; good for second-order impact checking.

Disadvantage

Can become overly complex; qualitative.

Overlays method McHarg, 1968–69

  • Uses a set of maps of the project area’s environmental features (wetlands, slopes, soil, wildlife habitats etc.) at the same scale as the project site plan.
  • Older technique: transparent plastic sheets in different colors.
  • Newer technique: GIS (Geographic Information Systems).
Advantage

Easy to understand, good display method, useful for site selection.

Disadvantage

Addresses only direct impacts; ignores duration/probability.

Socio-economic impact assessment

Nov 2025 · Nov 2024 · Apr 2025 · Dec 2022 — b)-part or paired question

Socio-economic impact assessment examines how a proposed project affects the social fabric and economic conditions of the people living in and around the project area. It covers changes in population, culture, livelihood, employment, and the local economy, and is a key part of EIA because these impacts directly affect human well-being, not just the physical environment.

Social impacts — four main heads

  • Demographic — displacement and relocation effects, changes in population characteristics.
  • Cultural — effects on traditional patterns, family structure, religious/archaeological features, and social networks.
  • Gender — implication of the project on roles of women, employment opportunity, and equity.
  • Institutional — effects on housing, schools, criminal justice, health, and welfare systems.

Economic impacts — assessed by examining

  • Duration of construction and operation phases.
  • Workforce requirements for each phase/period.
  • Skill requirements and local availability of skilled labour.
  • Earnings generated for the local population.
  • Raw material and input purchases from local sources.
  • Capital investment brought into the region.
  • Outputs of the project (goods/services produced).
  • Characteristics of the local economy (existing economic base, dependency patterns).

Steps / how it is assessed

01
Baseline data collectionStudy existing socio-economic conditions — population, occupation, income levels, infrastructure — before the project starts
02
Identification of affected groupsWho will be displaced, relocated, or whose livelihood will change
03
Prediction of impactsProfessional judgement, case studies, public consultation/opinion surveys to estimate magnitude of change
04
Evaluation of significanceRanked on importance of affected community, magnitude/extent, duration, reversibility — e.g. permanent displacement vs temporary job loss
05
Mitigation and compensationR&R plans, skill training, compensation packages, community development programs
Key purpose To ensure the project doesn’t disproportionately harm vulnerable or local populations, and to maximize positive economic benefits (employment, income, infrastructure) while minimizing social disruption (displacement, cultural loss, inequity).
Conclusion line — optional for the exam Socio-economic impact assessment is essential because it links the environmental and human dimensions of a project — a technically sound project can still fail or face opposition if its social and economic impacts on local communities are not properly assessed and mitigated.

Prediction & assessment of impacts on the water environment

Nov 2025 · Apr 2025 · Dec 2022

Water is one of the most vital resources affected by developmental projects. Water pollution is the contamination of water sources by substances that make water unusable for drinking, cooking, cleaning, or other activities, and disrupts aquatic ecosystems. Assessing impacts on the water environment involves identifying pollution sources, studying existing water quality/quantity, and predicting changes due to the proposed project.

Sources of water pollution — two types

  • Point sources — a single, identifiable source such as a pipe or drain (e.g. industrial waste discharged directly into rivers/sea).
  • Nonpoint sources — also called “diffuse pollution”; occur over a wide area, not traceable to one source; linked to land-use patterns (e.g. sediments, toxics, plastics from agricultural runoff).

Water quality parameters

  • Physical — e.g. turbidity, colour, temperature.
  • Chemical — e.g. pH, hardness, chlorides, heavy metals, nitrates.
  • Biological — e.g. presence of indicator organisms.
  • Compared against standards like IS: 10500 (Indian Standard Specifications for Drinking Water).

Water quality assessment — desktop study

  • Use of existing information (if available) from environmental protection agencies.
  • Data on chemical pollutants and biological surveys used to assess water chemistry.
  • Routine monitoring data of bathing waters and surface waters used for drinking.

Water quantity assessment — field study

  • Temporary stream gauging to determine flow regime (can be expensive).
  • Stream gauging methods for measuring discharge.
  • Detailed survey data fed into hydraulic models.
  • Surveys of rivers, biological/fisheries surveys, use of freshwater organisms as pollutant indicators, and biological monitoring methods.

General steps / procedure for impact assessment

01
Delineation of study area
02
Identification of project activitiesThose that will impact water quality/quantity
03
Preparation of baseline descriptionExisting water resource condition
04
Procurement of relevant standardsWater quality/quantity criteria, e.g. IS:10500
05
Impact predictionModels, mass balance, or professional judgement
06
Assessment of impact significanceMagnitude, duration, reversibility
07
Identification and incorporation of mitigation measures

Conclusion line — optional for the exam A combination of desktop study (quality) and field study (quantity), compared against statutory water quality standards, forms the backbone of water environment impact prediction — ensuring that project-induced pollution or depletion does not compromise water’s usability for human and ecological needs.

Prediction & assessment of impacts on the air environment

Nov 2025 · Dec 2023 · Dec 2022

Many developmental activities add air pollutants to the atmosphere or alter weather patterns, causing adverse effects on people, plants, animals, materials, and buildings — effects that can occur at local, regional, or even global scale. Assessing air quality impact involves identifying pollution sources, studying baseline air quality, and predicting pollutant concentrations using scientific and modelling approaches.

Air pollution sources — area and point, five categories

  • Transportation sources — motor vehicles, aircraft, trains, vessels, fuel evaporation losses.
  • Fuel combustion in stationary sources — residential, commercial, industrial fuel use, power plants.
  • Emission from industrial process losses — chemical, metallurgical, mineral, petroleum industries.
  • Solid waste disposal — incineration, open burning.
  • Miscellaneous — forest fires, structural fires, agricultural burning.

Effects of air pollution

  • Human health — eye irritation, headaches, aggravation of respiratory problems.
  • Plants/crops — abnormal growth, leaf discoloration/spotting, death.
  • Animals — undesirable consequences from atmospheric fluorides.
  • Materials damage — property devaluation, deterioration of concrete, discoloration of painted surfaces.
  • Climatic effects — greenhouse effect, ozone depletion.
  • Aesthetic effects — reduced visibility, photochemical smog, odour/dust nuisance.

Predicting changes in concentration

  • Assessment focuses on determining concentrations of air pollutants, compared against national/local air quality standards (e.g. WHO guidelines — SO₂, CO, NO₂, ozone, particulates).
  • Pre-project data collection determines pollutant concentration at different times and locations to establish a baseline.
  • This baseline data also feeds into predictive models, and in cases of higher-order effects, into predictions of deposition rates affecting flora, fauna, and local climate/visibility.

Six-step generalized approach

01
Sources, types & quantitiesEvaluation/identification of pollutants from each project phase — construction, operation
02
Existing ambient air qualityDetailed evaluation of air quality, meteorological conditions, and emission inventory of the area
03
Laws and standardsExamination of applicable laws, regulations, and emission standards
04
Impact assessmentMass balance, dispersion calculations, mathematical models, and/or qualitative predictions — case studies, professional judgement
05
Significance of impactsAssessment of anticipated beneficial and detrimental impacts
06
Mitigation / remedial measuresDevelopment of appropriate measures

Conclusion line — optional for the exam Air impact prediction relies on a mix of mathematical dispersion modelling and qualitative judgement, benchmarked against statutory air quality standards — allowing planners to anticipate pollution levels before a project is built and design suitable mitigation measures in advance.

Leopold matrix

Nov 2024 — standalone

The Leopold Matrix, developed by Luna Leopold (1971), is the most well-known and widely used interaction matrix method for identifying and displaying the impacts of a project on the environment. It links project actions with environmental impacts in a simple grid format.

Structure

  • A large matrix with 100 project actions along the horizontal axis (columns) and 88 environmental characteristics/conditions along the vertical axis (rows) — giving 8,800 possible interactions.

Working

  • For every action–impact interaction, a diagonal line is drawn in the cell, and two numbers (scale 1–10) are entered:
    • Magnitude — the size/extent of the impact (with +/– sign for beneficial/adverse).
    • Importance — the significance of that impact (weightage).

Source note — a diagram of the matrix grid is referenced; your notes say mention only, so the grid is not drawn here

Purpose

  • Helps assess which project activities cause the most significant impacts, and on which environmental components.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages
  • Comprehensive — covers a very wide range of actions and impacts.
  • Links action to impact clearly; good for displaying results.
  • Useful for comparing alternatives.
Disadvantages
  • Highly subjective — magnitude/importance scores depend on individual judgement.
  • Does not distinguish direct vs indirect impacts well.
  • Risk of double-counting impacts; purely qualitative, not probabilistic.
  • Too large and complex for small projects.

Network method

Nov 2024 — standalone

The Network method is an extension of the matrix approach, used to trace the chain of cause-effect relationships triggered by a project — capturing not just direct impacts but also their secondary and tertiary consequences.

Concept

  • It takes the matrix approach further by accounting for primary as well as secondary impacts, building what is called an impact tree.

Purpose

  • Used to identify cause-effect linkages between project actions and resulting environmental changes, showing how one impact triggers another in sequence.

Key feature

  • Provides a visual description of linkages — showing identification of direct, indirect, short-term, and long-term environmental impacts as an interconnected network/tree diagram.

Source note — impact tree/network diagram referenced; not drawn here, per your notes

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages
  • Links action to impact clearly, like matrices.
  • Handles both direct and indirect impacts — a major improvement over simple matrices.
  • Useful (in simplified form) for checking second-order impacts.
Disadvantages
  • Can become overly complex if extended beyond a simplified version.
  • Still largely qualitative in nature.

Note for the exam — if asked together

Network method = Matrix + cause-effect chain tracing (primary → secondary → tertiary impacts)
