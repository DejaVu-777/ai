01

Evolution of EIA in India

15 marks Nov 2025Nov 2024Dec 2023Apr 2025Dec 2022

Asked in literally every paper — worth 7–9 marks every time. If you memorise nothing else in Module I, memorise this. Structure that's easy to reproduce in exam order and still hits every scoring point: phase-wise timeline → 1994 notification → 2006 notification → recent amendments / Draft 2020.

  1. 1976–77
    to 1993

    Phase 1 — Pre-statutory / administrative

    • Foundation laid in 1976–77, when the Planning Commission directed the Department of Science and Technology (DST) to examine river valley and hydroelectric projects from an environmental angle.
    • Scope later extended to all projects requiring clearance from the Public Investment Board (PIB).
    • Till 1994, environmental clearance (EC) was purely an administrative decision — no legislative/statutory backing, so compliance was weak and inconsistent.
  2. 1986

    Phase 2 — Statutory foundation: EPA 1986

    • The Bhopal Gas Tragedy (1984) was a major trigger for stronger environmental law in India.
    • This led to the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, enacted 23 May 1986, giving the central government legal power to regulate activities harming environmental quality.
    • The EPA became the legal umbrella under which EIA would later be made mandatory.
  3. 27 Jan
    1994

    Phase 3 — The EIA Notification, 1994 (first formal notification)

    • Issued on 27 January 1994 by the MoEF, under Section 3 of the EPA, 1986.
    • Made Environmental Clearance mandatory for 29–30 categories of projects listed in Schedule 1.
    • Marked the formal institutionalization of EIA as a legally binding requirement — ending the purely administrative approach.
    • Amended about 12 times to strengthen procedures, but faced criticism for unpredictable appraisal schedules, delays, and poor-quality EIA reports by consultants.
  4. 14 Sep
    2006

    Phase 4 — The EIA Notification, 2006 (major overhaul)

    • Notified on 14 September 2006, replacing the 1994 notification entirely.
    • Key reform: decentralization — Category A appraised at the national level (MoEF, through EAC); Category B at the state level (via SEIAA and SEAC).
    • Introduced a structured 4-stage process: Screening → Scoping → Public Consultation → Appraisal.
    • Made public hearing / public consultation a formal, mandatory part of the process (absent or weak in 1994).
    • Removed the requirement of a separate "Site Clearance" stage — merged into scoping.
  5. 2006
    onward

    Phase 5 — Post-2006 amendments and current scenario

    • Since 2006, 100+ amendments / office memorandums issued, with 2022–23 recording the highest number of changes.
    • Notable: compliance rating system for SEIAA, dilution of public hearing for legacy mining cases, and a move toward online submission/approval.
    • The Draft EIA Notification, 2020 attempted to consolidate all amendments but drew major legal and public controversy, especially over post-facto clearance (allowing clearance after a project has already started/operated) — currently under judicial and public scrutiny, not yet finalized.
Scoring trap Don't call the 1994 notification the "first EIA in India" without the qualifier — EIA existed administratively from 1976–77. 1994 is the first statutory notification.
Quick recall — write this on the rough sheet first

1976-77 (DST, admin) → 1986 (EPA, Bhopal trigger) → 1994 (first notification, 29 categories) → 2006 (Cat A/B, decentralized, 4 stages) → post-2006 (100+ amendments, Draft 2020 controversy)

02

Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

15 marks Nov 2025Dec 2023Apr 2025Dec 2022missing only Nov 2024

Structure plan: Background/Need → Enactment details → Objectives → Salient features (powers) → Powers of Central Govt (Sec 3–5) → Penalties → Conclusion. KTU splits marks roughly 2–3 background, 6–7 features/powers, 2–3 penalties, rest conclusion.

1. Background and need

  • Before 1986, India had only sector-specific / fragmented laws — Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972, Water Act 1974, Air Act 1981 — with no single comprehensive law covering the environment as a whole.
  • The Bhopal Gas Tragedy (1984), India's worst industrial disaster, exposed this legal gap and became the direct trigger for a unified environmental law.
  • It was also enacted to implement India's commitments from the 1972 Stockholm Conference on the Human Environment.

2. Enactment

  • Passed by Parliament under Article 253 of the Constitution (power to legislate for implementing international agreements).
  • Received Presidential assent on 23 May 1986; came into force on 19 November 1986.
  • Structured into 4 Chapters and 26 Sections; applicable to the whole of India.
  • Known as the "Umbrella Act" — a single overarching framework under which other environmental rules/notifications (including the EIA Notification) are issued, mainly via Section 3.

3. Objectives of the Act

  • To provide for the protection and improvement of the environment and prevention of hazards to human beings, other living creatures, plants and property.
  • To implement decisions of the Stockholm Conference, 1972.
  • To coordinate the activities of various regulatory agencies under existing laws (Water Act, Air Act, etc.) under one single authority.
  • To create authorities with adequate powers for environmental protection.

4. Salient features / powers of the Central Government (Sec 3–5)

  • Section 3 — General powers: the Central Government can take "all measures necessary" to protect and improve environmental quality — includes laying down standards for emissions/discharge, restricting areas for industries, and EIA-related notifications.
  • Section 4 — Appointment of officers: power to appoint officers to exercise powers under the Act.
  • Section 5 — Power to give directions: may issue directions to any person/officer/authority, including power to shut down, prohibit or regulate any industry/operation/process.
  • Sections 7 & 8: prohibit discharge of environmental pollutants beyond prescribed standards, and regulate handling of hazardous substances.
  • Section 10: empowers officers to enter and inspect any premises.
  • Section 15: lays down penalties for contravention — imprisonment up to 5 years or fine up to ₹1 lakh, or both, with additional fine for continuing violation.

5. Significance / conclusion

  • The principal / umbrella legislation of Indian environmental law: all other key frameworks — EIA Notification (1994, 2006), Hazardous Waste Rules, Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) notifications — derive their legal authority from this Act.
  • Filled the critical pre-1986 legislative vacuum and remains the backbone connecting environmental policy to enforceable law in India.
Quick recall

Bhopal 1984 → Article 253 → assent 23 May 1986, force 19 Nov 1986 → 4 Ch / 26 Sec → "Umbrella Act" → Sec 3 (general powers, basis for EIA) → Sec 5 (directions) → Sec 15 (penalty: 5 yr / ₹1 lakh)

03

Salient features of EIA Notification, 2006

15 marks Nov 2025Nov 2024Dec 2023

Structure plan: Background/legal basis → Category A/B classification → 4-stage process (the heart of this answer, ~7–8 marks) → Other key features → Comparison note with 1994. This is the order examiners score against most consistently.

1. Legal basis and context

  • Issued by the MoEF on 14 September 2006, under Section 3 of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.
  • Replaced the EIA Notification, 1994, mainly to fix its problems: poor-quality EIA reports, unpredictable appraisal schedules, excessive centralization.
  • Core philosophy: decentralization of environmental clearance (EC) power from the Centre to the States.

2. Categorization of projects (key feature)

  • Projects split into Category A and Category B based on spatial extent and intensity of potential impact (not type of project alone).
  • Category A: high-impact projects → appraised at the national level by the Expert Appraisal Committee (EAC); clearance granted by MoEF; no screening — EIA is mandatory.
  • Category B: lower-impact projects → appraised at the state level by SEIAA (State EIA Authority) and SEAC (State Expert Appraisal Committee).
    • Category B1 — requires a full EIA study.
    • Category B2 — exempted from detailed EIA; only needs environmental management conditions.
  • Safety clause: if a state has no duly constituted SEIAA/SEAC, a Category B project is automatically treated as Category A.

3. The four-stage EIA process (most important part — write in sequence)

  1. Stage 1

    Screening

    Applies only to Category B; SEAC decides whether it is B1 (needs EIA) or B2 (exempted). Category A skips this stage.

  2. Stage 2
    60 days

    Scoping

    EAC/SEAC finalizes the Terms of Reference (ToR) defining what the EIA study must cover; done within 60 days.

  3. Stage 3
    45 days

    Public consultation

    Comments collected from affected local communities and other stakeholders on the draft EIA report; mandatory for Category A and B1 (with a few exemptions); completed in 45 days.

  4. Stage 4
    ~105 days

    Appraisal

    Final detailed scrutiny by EAC/SEAC leading to a recommendation to grant or reject EC; completed in ~105 days.

Total statutory timeline Around 210 days for Category A / B1 projects.

4. Other salient features

  • Introduction of Expert Appraisal Committees (EAC) at the Centre and SEAC at the State level for systematic review.
  • Mandates transparency: draft EIA reports must be made available to the public before the hearing.
  • Validity of EC is time-bound (varies by project type — e.g. mining, river valley projects), after which renewal / fresh appraisal is needed.
  • Unlike 1994, 2006 explicitly provides standardized formats for application (Form 1) and ToR.

5. Significance

  • Marked the shift from a centralized, ad-hoc clearance system to a structured, decentralized, time-bound one.
  • However, criticised for 100+ amendments since 2006 (highest in 2022–23) and dilution of public hearing norms in certain cases (e.g. legacy mining projects).
Quick recall

14 Sept 2006, Sec 3 EPA → Cat A (Centre, EAC, no screening) vs Cat B (State, SEIAA/SEAC, B1/B2) → 4 stages: Screening (B only) → Scoping (60 d) → Public Consultation (45 d) → Appraisal (105 d) → total ~210 days → 100+ post-2006 amendments

Environmental standards — water, air, noise

15 marks Nov 2025Apr 2025Dec 2022

Three media, so split marks roughly 5-5-5. Each medium gets: governing law/authority → standard type(s) → key numeric limits → purpose.

A. Water quality standards

  • Governed by the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974, enforced by CPCB (Central) and SPCB (State).
  • Two main standard types:
    • Drinking water — IS 10500 (BIS): specifies an Acceptable Limit and a Permissible Limit (used only when no alternate source exists) for parameters like pH 6.5–8.5, TDS (desirable 500 mg/l, permissible 2000 mg/l), total hardness, fluoride (1.5 mg/l max) and E. coli (must be absent / 100 ml).
    • Designated Best Use Classification of Inland Surface Water (CPCB) — rivers/streams in 5 classes by intended use.

05

Water (Prevention & Control of Pollution) Act, 1974

15 marks Nov 2024Apr 2025

Structure plan: Background/Constitutional basis → Objectives → Structure (Chapters/Sections) → CPCB & SPCB powers and functions (the fattest section, ~6–7 marks) → Consent mechanism → Penalties → Significance. The standard KTU scoring order for "Act" questions.

1. Background and constitutional basis

  • India's first dedicated environmental legislation, enacted as Act No. 6 of 1974, receiving assent on 23 March 1974.
  • Since "Water" is a State subject, Parliament could legislate nationally only because 12 state legislatures passed resolutions under Article 252, authorising the Centre to enact a uniform law.
  • Later extended/adopted by other states and applicable across the whole of India.

2. Objectives

  • To provide for the prevention and control of water pollution.
  • To maintain or restore the wholesomeness (natural purity) of water bodies — rivers, lakes, ponds, groundwater.
  • To establish Boards (Central and State) for carrying out these purposes and conferring powers on them to implement the Act.

3. Structure of the Act

  • 8 Chapters and 64 Sections.
  • Key chapters: Ch. II — Constitution of Central/State Boards; Ch. IV — Powers & Functions of Boards; Ch. V — Prevention and Control of Water Pollution (consent mechanism); Ch. VII — Penalties and Procedure.

4. Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) — Sec 3 & 16

  • Constituted in September 1974 by the Central Government as a statutory, apex body.
  • Key functions (Section 16):
    • Advises the Central Government on prevention and control of water pollution.
    • Coordinates the activities of State Boards and resolves disputes among them.
    • Lays down, modifies and reviews standards for streams/wells; coordinates research and training.
    • Later given powers under the Air Act, 1981 too — making it the central nodal body for both water and air.

5. State Pollution Control Board (SPCB) — Sec 4 & 17

  • Constituted by State Governments; functions at the state/UT level, operating under CPCB's directions.
  • Key functions (Section 17):
    • Advises the state government on siting of industries to avoid pollution of water bodies.
    • Plans and executes programmes for prevention, control or abatement of water pollution.
    • Inspects sewage/trade-effluent treatment plants and lays down effluent standards.
    • Grants, refuses or cancels "Consent" for establishing/operating an industry.

6. The consent mechanism (Sec 25 & 26) — operational heart of the Act

  • Any person/industry intending to establish a plant or process likely to discharge sewage or trade effluent into a stream, well, sewer or land must obtain prior "Consent to Establish / Operate" (CTE/CTO) from the SPCB.
  • Consent is granted for a fixed period, subject to conditions (installation of ETP, compliance with effluent standards); can be refused or revoked if a unit crosses permissible pollution limits.
  • This remains the primary regulatory tool through which the Act is enforced on the ground even today.

7. Penalties

  • Establishing or operating an industry without consent, or contravening Board directions → imprisonment up to 6 years and fine, with continuing-offence provisions for ongoing violations.

8. Significance / related developments

  • Supplemented by the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Cess Act, 1977 — a cess on water consumption by polluting industries to fund Board activities — repealed in 2017 after GST was introduced.
  • Provided the scientific/legal backbone for later programmes like the Ganga Action Plan and Namami Gange.
  • The Water (Amendment) Bill, 2024 proposes to decriminalize minor offences and allow certain categories of plants to be exempted from statutory restrictions — a recent reform still under discussion.
Quick recall

Act 6/1974, Art. 252 (12 states) → 8 Ch / 64 Sec → objective: prevent pollution + restore wholesomeness → CPCB (Sec 16, advises Centre, coordinates states) + SPCB (Sec 17, advises state, grants consent) → consent mechanism (Sec 25–26, CTE/CTO) → penalty 6 yrs + fine → Cess Act 1977 (repealed 2017, GST) → 2024 Amendment Bill

06

Air (Prevention & Control of Pollution) Act, 1981

15 marks Nov 2024Dec 2022

Same skeleton as the Water Act, so they're easy to compare in the exam: Background → Objectives → Structure → Boards & functions → Air Pollution Control Area + consent → Penalties → Relation to Water Act / 1987 amendment.

1. Background

  • Enacted to implement India's commitment from the Stockholm Conference, 1972, specifically to tackle air pollution, which the Water Act, 1974 did not cover.
  • Came into force on 16 May 1981 (passed under the Union's residuary / implementing-international-decisions power, similar basis as other post-Stockholm laws).
  • Preceded by the Water Act, 1974; later followed and complemented by the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.

2. Objectives

  • To prevent, control and abate air pollution in India.
  • To maintain the quality of air necessary for sustaining human health, animals, plants and property.
  • To establish Boards to carry out these functions and confer them with necessary powers.

3. Structure of the Act

  • 7 Chapters and 54 Sections.
  • Does not create new Boards — it designates the existing CPCB and SPCBs (already constituted under the Water Act, 1974) to additionally function as air pollution regulators.
  • In states with no SPCB under the Water Act, a new Board is constituted under Section 5 of the Air Act.

4. CPCB functions (central level)

  • Advises the Central Government on improvement of air quality and prevention/control of air pollution.
  • Plans and executes nationwide programmes for air pollution control.
  • Coordinates activities of SPCBs and resolves disputes among them.
  • Lays down and revises National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) — the statutory link to the air standards topic.

5. SPCB functions (state level)

  • Executes CPCB's air pollution programmes at the state level.
  • Carries out inspections of air pollution control areas and industrial plants.
  • Advises the State Government on feasibility/location of industries from an air-pollution standpoint.
  • Lays down emission standards for industries within the state.

6. Air Pollution Control Area + consent mechanism (core operational provisions)

  • Section 19: the State Government can declare any area an "Air Pollution Control Area", after consultation with the SPCB.
  • Section 21: no person can establish or operate an industrial plant in such an area without prior consent of the SPCB — the key enforcement tool, structurally identical to the Water Act's consent mechanism.
  • Section 22: no industry operating in a control area can emit air pollutants in excess of standards laid down by the SPCB.

7. Penalties

  • Section 37: failure to obtain consent under Section 21, or violating consent conditions → imprisonment of 1.5 years to 6 years, with fine, and additional fine for continuing contravention.

8. Relation to Water Act & later amendment

  • The Air Act deliberately reused the Water Act's institutional machinery (CPCB/SPCB) instead of duplicating it — making the two Acts structurally parallel (both use the consent mechanism and Board-based enforcement).
  • The original 1981 Act did not cover noise pollution; added later via the 1987 Amendment, which brought noise under the definition of "air pollutant".
Quick recall

16 May 1981, post-Stockholm 1972 → 7 Ch / 54 Sec → reuses CPCB/SPCB from Water Act 1974 → CPCB: advises Centre, sets NAAQS | SPCB: executes, inspects, emission standards → Sec 19 (Air Pollution Control Area) → Sec 21 (prior consent) → Sec 22 (emission limit) → Sec 37 penalty (1.5–6 yrs + fine) → 1987 amendment added noise

08

Environmental Clearance procedure for a project

process question flowchart · steps

Before any project — nuclear, hydroelectric, mining, or any Category I/II activity — can be set up in India, it must obtain Environmental Clearance (EC) under the EIA Notification, 2006, issued by the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEF&CC). The process ensures environmental concerns are examined before the project begins, not after damage is done. It runs through four sequential stages leading to a final decision.

Flowchart — the EC process

Screening→Scoping→Public Consultation→Appraisal→Decision

Stage 1 — Screening

The project is checked to see whether it needs an EIA at all, and if so, at what level. Projects are classified into Category A (clearance from the Central Government, via MoEF&CC) and Category B (clearance from the State Government, via SEIAA — State Environment Impact Assessment Authority). Category B splits further into B1 (needs full EIA) and B2 (exempted from detailed EIA).

Stage 2 — Scoping

Once screened in, the Expert Appraisal Committee (EAC) or State Level EAC (SEAC) decides the Terms of Reference (ToR) — which environmental parameters (air, water, soil, noise, biodiversity, socio-economic impact) must be studied in detail for that specific project.

Baseline data collection / EIA report preparation

Based on the ToR, the project proponent conducts field studies — generally a Rapid EIA (one season of data, ~3 months) or a Comprehensive EIA (all four seasons, ~16 months) — and prepares the draft EIA report along with an Environmental Management Plan (EMP).

Stage 3 — Public consultation

This has two parts:

  • Public Hearing (PH): conducted at or near the project site by the State Pollution Control Board (SPCB); local affected persons and stakeholders raise concerns. A minimum 30-day notice is given before the hearing.
  • Written responses: comments collected from concerned persons who have a plausible stake in the project but could not attend.

After the hearing, the applicant must address all concerns raised and revise the draft EIA and EMP accordingly, then submit the Final EIA Report to the regulatory authority.

Stage 4 — Appraisal

The EAC/SEAC carries out a detailed scrutiny of the Final EIA report, the public hearing proceedings and the EMP, and recommends either grant or rejection of clearance to the regulatory authority (MoEF&CC for Category A, SEIAA for Category B).

Decision

Based on the EAC/SEAC recommendation, the Central or State Government grants or rejects EC, often with specific conditions attached — safeguards, mitigation measures, monitoring requirements.

Monitoring

Once the project is operational, compliance with EC conditions is periodically monitored — a continuous obligation, not a one-time formality.

Post-clearance audit and evaluation

The final stage checks whether the EIA predictions were accurate and whether the mitigation measures actually worked, recommending corrective action if needed.

Category III / exempted projects

Certain projects — defence road construction in border areas, pipeline projects, small-scale activities — are exempted from the EIA process altogether.

Key terms to remember Screening → Scoping → Public Consultation → Appraisal; EAC/SEAC, SPCB, SEIAA, MoEF&CC, EMP.

Different types of EIA

full question Nov 2025Dec 2023Apr 2025Dec 20224/5 — usually the OR question

EIA is not a single rigid technique — it is categorised by the level of planning, geographical scale and stage at which it is applied. Based on systematic analysis of environmental parameters, geographical region, carrying-capacity limitations and sectoral planning, it is broadly classified into five types: Strategic, Regional, Sectoral, Project-level and Life Cycle Assessment. The classification decides when and at what level environmental concerns enter decision-making.

1. Strategic EIA (SEIA)

Systematic analysis of the environmental effects of development policies, plans, programmes and other proposed strategic actions — not individual projects. It extends the aims of EIA upstream in decision-making, applied before project-level decisions are made, while major alternatives are still open. A proactive approach that integrates environmental considerations into higher-level policy decisions.

2. Regional EIA

Integrates environmental concerns into development planning for a geographic region (usually sub-country level) — an approach called Economic-cum-Environmental (EcE) development planning. It combines economic development with management of renewable natural resources within carrying-capacity limits, to achieve sustainable development.

Regional EIA fulfils the need for macro-level environmental integration that project-level EIA cannot achieve alone: it sets the context for individual project EIAs in that region, and lets the cumulative impacts of all projects there be accounted for together.

3. Sectoral EIA

Instead of one project in isolation, EIA is applied to an entire sector's development plan — e.g. all power projects, all mining projects in a sector. Once sector-level plans already address environmental concerns, the scope of subsequent project-level EIA becomes much narrower. It addresses the specific environmental problems typical of planning and implementing that sector's projects.

4. Project-level EIA

The most common and traditional form — assesses a single developmental activity in isolation, examining the impacts it exerts on the immediate receiving environment. Its major limitation: it does not effectively capture cumulative effects of multiple developments happening together in a region.

5. Life Cycle Assessment (LCA)

Evaluates the environmental impact of a project or product across its entire life cycle — raw material extraction, construction/manufacturing and operation, through to decommissioning and disposal — rather than at one stage only.

Why the classification matters Moving from Strategic → Regional → Sectoral → Project-level is moving from broad policy-level thinking down to a single project. Each level catches impacts the level below it would miss — for example the cumulative, regional impacts a single project-level EIA cannot see.

Initial Environmental Examination (IEE)

b) part · ~7–8 marks Nov 2025Dec 2023Apr 2025paired with types of EIA

An early, low-cost, screening-level study carried out to decide whether a proposed project has the potential to cause significant environmental impacts, and if so whether a full-scale EIA is actually required. It sits right after the Screening stage and before a full EIA is commissioned — a filter that saves time, money and resources by avoiding detailed studies for low-risk projects.

IEE in the overall EIA process

Project Screening→IEE→Full-Scale EIA

Definition and purpose

  • Definition: a low-cost environmental evaluation that uses information already available — existing data, reports, maps — rather than generating fresh, detailed field data.
  • Purpose: it would be time-consuming and wasteful for every proposed project to undergo a full EIA; since not all developments pose a genuine threat, IEE filters out low-risk projects early.
  • Core function: determines (a) whether a project requires a full EIA at all, and (b) if so, what level of environmental review is necessary.
  • Role in the process: IEE speeds up the overall EIA process by resolving simple issues quickly and directing only genuinely significant ones for detailed assessment.

Four key functions of IEE

  1. Identifies potential significant environmental issues associated with the project.
  2. Grades the effects and pinpoints the actual Significant Environmental Issues (SEIs).
  3. Resolves the simple/minor SEIs directly at this stage itself.
  4. Recommends further action for the outstanding, more serious SEIs.

Outcome of the IEE review — two paths

All SEIs resolved

The project proceeds according to the terms laid out in the IEE itself — no full EIA needed.

Significant issues remain

The process moves to Scoping, which lays down the Terms of Reference (ToR) for a Full-Scale EIA.

Relationship with scoping and the full-scale EIA

IEE feeds directly into Scoping: the outstanding SEIs identified in the IEE become the input for the Full-Scale EIA's assessment phase.

Significance IEE reflects the "screen before you study" philosophy of EIA — avoiding both mistakes: ignoring a genuinely risky project, or wasting resources studying a harmless one in full detail.

Terms of Reference (ToR)

short note · 2–3 or 7 marks contents / relevance

The document that emerges from the Scoping stage. It defines the exact boundaries, issues and work plan for the detailed (Full-Scale) EIA study that follows — telling the project proponent "what to study, how much to study, and how to present it." Without a clear ToR, an EIA study becomes either too broad (wasting time and money) or too narrow (missing real impacts).

Origin

Finalised during the Scoping stage — a process of interaction between government agencies and project proponents — once the IEE Review confirms that outstanding Significant Environmental Issues (SEIs) remain unresolved and a Full-Scale EIA is required.

Core purpose — scoping (and hence the ToR) identifies

  • Spatial and temporal boundaries for the EIA — what geographic area and what time period the study should cover.
  • Important issues and concerns relevant to the specific project.
  • Information necessary for decision-making.
  • Significant effects and factors that must be considered in the study.

Main contents of the ToR

  1. Background — context of the project, its location and nature.
  2. Impact issues —
    • Significant issues to be studied in depth.
    • Relevant resources — environmental components such as air, water, soil, biodiversity, socio-economic — that need assessment.
    • Report formatting — how the final EIA report should be structured and presented.
  3. Work plan — the When / Who / How of task completion: timeline, responsible agencies/experts, and methodology.

Function and position in the process

The ToR establishes the terms of reference for the Full-Scale EIA: the detailed study must address strictly the issues and boundaries the ToR laid down, nothing vague or open-ended.

IEE→IEE Review→Scoping / ToR→Full-Scale EIA

Relevance / importance

  • Prevents wastage of time and resources by focusing the EIA only on genuinely significant issues.
  • Ensures all stakeholders (government + proponent) agree in advance on the scope, avoiding disputes later.
  • Provides a clear, structured work plan, so the Full-Scale EIA is systematic rather than haphazard.
  • Acts as a reference/checklist during Appraisal — the EAC/SEAC checks whether the final EIA report actually followed the approved ToR.
Exam tip Short note (2–3 marks): definition + where it comes from (Scoping) + the three main contents (Background, Impact Issues, Work Plan). Full 7 marks: add the spatial/temporal boundaries point and the relevance bullets.

Generic structure of an EIA report

13–14 marks Apr 2025Dec 2022

The formal document prepared by the project proponent (through accredited consultants), summarising the whole assessment from baseline data to proposed mitigation. As per the EIA Notification, 2006 guidelines every EIA report follows a standard generic structure, so that the EAC/SEAC can review it uniformly and the public can understand it during Public Consultation. Contents vary slightly by project type (nuclear, mining, hydroelectric), but the skeleton stays the same.

  1. Introduction — purpose of the report, identification of the project and its proponent, scope of the study, and the name of the organisation preparing the EIA.
  2. Project description — nature, size and location of the project; process/technology involved; raw materials and resource requirements (land, water, power); project schedule and cost.
  3. Description of the environment (baseline data) — the existing environmental status of the study area: air, water, soil, noise, biological (flora/fauna) and socio-economic components, collected through Rapid or Comprehensive EIA field studies.
  4. Anticipated environmental impacts & mitigation measures — identifies and predicts likely direct and indirect impacts on each environmental component (human beings, flora/fauna, soil/water/air, climate, material assets, cultural heritage), and proposes measures to prevent, minimise or offset them.
  5. Analysis of alternatives (technology & site) — compares the chosen site/technology against viable alternatives, including the "no project" option, and justifies the final selection on environmental grounds.
  6. Environmental monitoring programme — the plan for post-clearance monitoring: which parameters will be tracked, at what frequency, and by which agency, to verify that actual impacts match predictions.
  7. Additional studies — specialised studies where relevant: Risk Assessment (hazard-prone industries), Social Impact Assessment, or a Rehabilitation & Resettlement (R&R) Plan if displacement is involved.
  8. Project benefits — the positive outcomes: infrastructure improvement, employment generation, socio-economic development of the region.
  9. Environmental Management Plan (EMP) — the most critical section: a detailed action plan for mitigating/controlling adverse impacts and bringing them within prescribed standards (as laid down by CPCB and MoEF&CC), including costs and implementation schedule.
  10. Summary & conclusion — a non-technical summary of the whole report, understandable to the general public and decision-makers (this portion is also used during the Public Hearing).
  11. Disclosure of consultants — name and credentials of the accredited EIA consultant/agency who prepared the report, mandated for accountability and quality control.
Exam tip "Generic structure / contents of EIA report" → list all 11 points for the full 13–14 marks. As a short note, compress to: Introduction → Project Description → Baseline Environment → Anticipated Impacts → Alternatives Analysis → EMP → Monitoring Plan → Summary — 7–8 headings are enough to show you know the standard format.
