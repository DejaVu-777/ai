Topic 01 · 15 marks

Evolution of EIA in India

Flagged as a guaranteed repeat · no year list in the source

Phase 1 · 1976–77 to 1993

Pre-statutory / administrative phase

  • The foundation of EIA in India was laid in 1976–77, when the Planning Commission directed the Department of Science and Technology (DST) to examine river valley and hydroelectric projects from an environmental angle.
  • This scope was later extended to all projects requiring clearance from the Public Investment Board (PIB).
  • Till 1994, environmental clearance (EC) was purely an administrative decision — it had no legislative/statutory backing, so compliance was weak and inconsistent.

Phase 2 · 1986

Statutory foundation — Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

  • The Bhopal Gas Tragedy (1984) was a major trigger for stronger environmental law in India.
  • This led to the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 (EPA), enacted on 23 May 1986, which gave the central government legal power to regulate activities harming environmental quality.
  • The EPA became the legal umbrella under which EIA would later be made mandatory.

Phase 3 · 1994

The EIA Notification, 1994 (first formal notification)

  • Issued on 27 January 1994 by the MoEF, under Section 3 of the EPA, 1986.
  • Made Environmental Clearance (EC) mandatory for 29–30 categories of projects listed in Schedule 1.
  • This marked the formal institutionalization of EIA as a legally binding requirement — ending the earlier purely administrative approach.
  • The 1994 notification was amended about 12 times over the years to strengthen procedures, but faced criticism for unpredictable appraisal schedules, delays, and poor-quality EIA reports by consultants.

Phase 4 · 2006

The EIA Notification, 2006 (major overhaul)

  • Notified on 14 September 2006, replacing the 1994 notification entirely.
  • Key reform: decentralization — projects classified into:
    • Category A → appraised at the national level (MoEF, through EAC)
    • Category B → appraised at the state level (via SEIAA and SEAC)
  • Introduced a structured 4-stage process: Screening → Scoping → Public Consultation → Appraisal.
  • Made public hearing / public consultation a formal, mandatory part of the process (a feature absent/weak in 1994).
  • Removed the requirement of a separate “Site Clearance” stage — merged into scoping.

Phase 5 · post-2006

Post-2006 amendments and current scenario

  • Since 2006, there have been 100+ amendments/office memorandums issued, with 2022–23 recording the highest number of changes.
  • Notable changes: compliance rating system for SEIAA, dilution of public hearing for legacy mining cases, and move toward online submission/approval.
  • The Draft EIA Notification, 2020 attempted to consolidate all amendments but drew major legal and public controversy, especially over post-facto clearance (allowing clearance after a project has already started/operated) — currently under judicial and public scrutiny, not yet finalized.

Quick-recall skeleton — write this on the rough sheet first

1976-77 (DST, admin) → 1986 (EPA, Bhopal trigger) → 1994 (first notification, 29 categories) → 2006 (Cat A/B, decentralized, 4 stages) → post-2006 (100+ amendments, Draft 2020 controversy)

Topic 02 · 15 marks

Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

Nov 2025 · Dec 2023 · Apr 2025 · Dec 2022 — only missing from Nov 2024

Structure plan — how KTU splits the marks Background/Need → Enactment details → Objectives → Salient features (powers) → Powers of Central Govt (Sec 3–5) → Penalties → Conclusion. Roughly 2–3 marks background, 6–7 features/powers, 2–3 penalties, the rest conclusion.

1 · Background and need

  • Before 1986, India had only sector-specific/fragmented laws — Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972, Water Act 1974, Air Act 1981 — with no single comprehensive law covering the environment as a whole.
  • The Bhopal Gas Tragedy (1984), India’s worst industrial disaster, exposed this legal gap and became the direct trigger for a unified environmental law.
  • It was also enacted to implement India’s commitments from the 1972 Stockholm Conference on the Human Environment.

2 · Enactment

  • Passed by Parliament under Article 253 of the Constitution (power to legislate for implementing international agreements).
  • Received Presidential assent on 23 May 1986 and came into force on 19 November 1986.
  • Structured into 4 Chapters and 26 Sections; applicable to the whole of India.
  • Known as the “Umbrella Act” since it provides a single overarching framework under which other environmental rules/notifications (including the EIA Notification) are issued, mainly via Section 3.

3 · Objectives of the Act

  • To provide for the protection and improvement of the environment and prevention of hazards to human beings, other living creatures, plants, and property.
  • To implement decisions of the Stockholm Conference, 1972.
  • To coordinate the activities of various regulatory agencies under existing laws (Water Act, Air Act, etc.) under one single authority.
  • To create authorities with adequate powers for environmental protection.

4 · Salient features / powers of Central Government (Sec 3–5)

  • Section 3 — General powers: Central Government can take “all measures necessary” to protect and improve environmental quality — includes laying down standards for emissions/discharge, restricting areas for industries, and EIA-related notifications.
  • Section 4 — Appointment of officers: power to appoint officers to exercise powers under the Act.
  • Section 5 — Power to give directions: Central Government may issue directions to any person/officer/authority, including power to shut down, prohibit, or regulate any industry/operation/process.
  • Sections 7 & 8: prohibit discharge of environmental pollutants beyond prescribed standards, and regulate handling of hazardous substances.
  • Section 10: empowers officers to enter and inspect any premises.
  • Section 15: lays down penalties for contravention — imprisonment up to 5 years or fine up to ₹1 lakh, or both, with additional fine for continuing violation.

5 · Significance / conclusion

  • It is the principal/umbrella legislation of Indian environmental law, since all other key frameworks — the EIA Notification (1994, 2006), Hazardous Waste Rules, Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) notifications — derive their legal authority from this Act.
  • It filled the critical pre-1986 legislative vacuum and remains the backbone connecting environmental policy to enforceable law in India.

Quick-recall skeleton

Bhopal 1984 → Article 253 → assent 23 May 1986, force 19 Nov 1986 → 4 Ch/26 Sec → “Umbrella Act” → Sec 3 (general powers, basis for EIA) → Sec 5 (directions) → Sec 15 (penalty: 5yr/₹1 lakh)

Topic 03 · 15 marks

Salient features of EIA Notification, 2006

Nov 2025 · Nov 2024 · Dec 2023

Structure plan — order examiners score against Background/legal basis → Category A/B classification → 4-stage process (the heart of the answer, ~7–8 marks) → Other key features → Comparison note with 1994.

1 · Legal basis and context

  • Issued by the MoEF on 14 September 2006, under Section 3 of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.
  • Replaced the EIA Notification, 1994, mainly to fix its problems: poor-quality EIA reports, unpredictable appraisal schedules, and excessive centralization.
  • Core philosophy: decentralization of environmental clearance (EC) power from the Centre to the States.

2 · Categorization of projects (key feature)

  • Projects are split into Category A and Category B based on spatial extent and intensity of potential impact (not type of project alone).
  • Category A: high-impact projects → appraised at the national level by the Expert Appraisal Committee (EAC); clearance granted by MoEF; no screening — EIA is mandatory.
  • Category B: lower-impact projects → appraised at the state level by SEIAA (State EIA Authority) and SEAC (State Expert Appraisal Committee).
    • Category B1: requires a full EIA study.
    • Category B2: exempted from detailed EIA; only needs environmental management conditions.
Safety clause If a state has no duly constituted SEIAA/SEAC, a Category B project is automatically treated as Category A.

3 · The four-stage EIA process — write in sequence

  • Stage 1 — Screening: applies only to Category B projects; SEAC decides whether it is B1 (needs EIA) or B2 (exempted). Category A skips this stage.
  • Stage 2 — Scoping: EAC/SEAC finalizes the Terms of Reference (ToR) defining what issues the EIA study must cover; done within 60 days.
  • Stage 3 — Public Consultation: comments collected from affected local communities and other stakeholders on the draft EIA report; mandatory for Category A and B1 (with a few exemptions); completed in 45 days.
  • Stage 4 — Appraisal: final detailed scrutiny by EAC/SEAC leading to a recommendation to grant or reject EC; completed in ~105 days.
  • Total statutory timeline: around 210 days for Category A/B1 projects.

4 · Other salient features

  • Introduction of Expert Appraisal Committees (EAC) at the Centre and SEAC at the State level for systematic review.
  • Mandates transparency: draft EIA reports must be made available to the public before the hearing.
  • Validity of Environmental Clearance is time-bound (varies by project type — e.g. mining, river valley projects), after which renewal/fresh appraisal is needed.
  • Unlike 1994, 2006 explicitly provides standardized formats for application (Form 1) and ToR.

5 · Significance

  • Marked the shift from a centralized, ad-hoc clearance system to a structured, decentralized, and time-bound one.
  • However, has faced criticism for 100+ amendments since 2006 (highest in 2022–23) and dilution of public hearing norms in certain cases (e.g. legacy mining projects).

Quick-recall skeleton

14 Sept 2006, Sec 3 EPA → Cat A (Centre, EAC, no screening) vs Cat B (State, SEIAA/SEAC, B1/B2) → 4 stages: Screening (B only) → Scoping (60d) → Public Consultation (45d) → Appraisal (105d) → total ~210 days → 100+ post-2006 amendments

Topic 04 · 15 marks

Environmental standards — water, air, and noise

Nov 2025 · Apr 2025 · Dec 2022

Structure plan — three media, ~5 marks each Each medium gets: governing law/authority → standard type(s) → key numeric limits → purpose. Mirrors how KTU splits multi-part environmental-science questions.

A · Water quality standards (≈5 marks)

  • Governed by the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974, enforced by CPCB (Central) and SPCB (State level).
  • Two main standard types:
    • Drinking water standards — IS 10500 (BIS): specifies Acceptable Limit and Permissible Limit (used only when no alternate source exists) for parameters like pH (6.5–8.5), TDS (desirable 500 mg/l, permissible 2000 mg/l), Total Hardness, Fluoride (1.5 mg/l max), and E. coli (must be absent/100 ml).
    • Designated Best Use Classification of Inland Surface Water (CPCB) — rivers/streams classified into 5 classes based on intended use:
      • Class A – drinking water source without conventional treatment (only disinfection); DO ≥ 6 mg/l, BOD ≤ 2 mg/l.
      • Class B – outdoor organized bathing; DO ≥ 5 mg/l, BOD ≤ 3 mg/l.
      • Class C – drinking water source with conventional treatment + disinfection.
      • Class D – propagation of wildlife and fisheries.
      • Class E – irrigation, industrial cooling, controlled waste disposal.
  • Effluent discharge standards also exist separately for industries discharging into inland surface water, public sewers, land for irrigation, and marine/coastal areas — each with different permissible limits for BOD, suspended solids, pH, etc.

B · Air quality standards (≈5 marks)

  • Governed by the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981; CPCB empowered to lay down and revise air quality standards.
  • Current National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) notified on 18 November 2009, replacing earlier 1994 and 1998 versions — made standards uniform across industrial, residential and rural areas (earlier these were less strict for industrial zones).
  • Covers 12 pollutants; key limits (annual mean, µg/m³):
    • SO₂: 50 (industrial/residential), 20 (ecologically sensitive areas)
    • NO₂: 40 (industrial/residential), 30 (sensitive areas)
    • PM10: 60 annual / 100 (24-hr)
    • PM2.5: 40 annual / 60 (24-hr)
    • Ozone: 100 (8-hr) / 180 (1-hr)
    • Lead: 0.5 µg/m³ (annual)
  • The National Air Quality Index (AQI), launched October 2014, translates these 8 key pollutants (PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, CO, O3, NH3, Pb) into color-coded categories for easy public understanding — AQI is real-time/short-term, while NAAQS is the regulatory/long-term legal benchmark.

C · Noise standards (≈5 marks)

  • Noise was first brought under the Air Act, 1981 (as an “air pollutant” via the 1987 amendment), then separately governed by the Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000.
  • States must categorize areas into 4 zones — industrial, commercial, residential, silence zone — each with day (6 AM–10 PM) and night (10 PM–6 AM) limits in dB(A) Leq:

  • Silence Zone = area within 100 m of hospitals, educational institutions, courts, and religious places — firecrackers and construction equipment banned here, especially at night.
  • Loudspeakers/PA systems are restricted between 10 PM and 6 AM, except in closed premises with special permission.

Quick-recall skeleton

Water → Water Act 1974, IS 10500 (BIS, drinking), CPCB Classes A–E (best-use) | Air → Air Act 1981, NAAQS 2009 (12 pollutants, PM10=60/100, PM2.5=40/60, SO2=50/20) | Noise → Noise Rules 2000, 4 zones (I-75/70, C-65/55, R-55/45, S-50/40), silence zone=100m

Topic 05 · 15 marks

Water (Prevention & Control of Pollution) Act, 1974

Nov 2024 · Apr 2025

Structure plan — standard KTU order for “Act” questions Background/Constitutional basis → Objectives → Structure (Chapters/Sections) → CPCB & SPCB (powers/functions — the fattest section, ~6–7 marks) → Consent mechanism → Penalties → Significance.

1 · Background and constitutional basis

  • India’s first dedicated environmental legislation, enacted as Act No. 6 of 1974, receiving assent on 23 March 1974.
  • Since “Water” is a State subject under the Constitution, Parliament could legislate on it nationally only because 12 state legislatures passed resolutions under Article 252, authorizing the Centre to enact a uniform law.
  • Later extended/adopted by other states and applicable across the whole of India.

2 · Objectives of the Act

  • To provide for the prevention and control of water pollution.
  • To maintain or restore the wholesomeness (natural purity) of water bodies — rivers, lakes, ponds, groundwater.
  • To establish Boards (Central and State) for carrying out these purposes and conferring powers on them to implement the Act.

3 · Structure of the Act

  • Comprises 8 Chapters and 64 Sections.
  • Key chapters: Ch. II — constitution of Central/State Boards; Ch. IV — powers & functions of Boards; Ch. V — prevention and control of water pollution (consent mechanism); Ch. VII — penalties and procedure.

4 · Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) — Sec 3 & 16

  • Constituted in September 1974 by the Central Government as a statutory, apex body.
  • Key functions (Section 16):
    • Advises the Central Government on matters relating to prevention and control of water pollution.
    • Coordinates the activities of State Boards and resolves disputes among them.
    • Lays down, modifies, and reviews standards for streams/wells and coordinates research/training.
    • Later also given powers and functions under the Air (Prevention & Control of Pollution) Act, 1981 — making it the central nodal body for both water and air.

5 · State Pollution Control Board (SPCB) — Sec 4 & 17

  • Constituted by State Governments; functions at the state/UT level, operating under CPCB’s directions.
  • Key functions (Section 17):
    • Advises the state government on siting of industries to avoid pollution of water bodies.
    • Plans and executes programmes for prevention, control, or abatement of water pollution.
    • Inspects sewage/trade-effluent treatment plants and lays down effluent standards.
    • Grants, refuses, or cancels “Consent” for establishing/operating an industry (see below).

6 · The consent mechanism (Sec 25 & 26) — operational heart

  • Any person/industry intending to establish a plant or process likely to discharge sewage or trade effluent into a stream, well, sewer, or land must obtain prior “Consent to Establish/Operate” (CTE/CTO) from the SPCB.
  • Consent is granted for a fixed period, subject to conditions (installation of ETP, compliance with effluent standards); can be refused or revoked if a unit crosses permissible pollution limits.
  • This remains the primary regulatory tool through which the Act is enforced on the ground even today.

7 · Penalties

  • Establishing or operating an industry without consent, or contravening Board directions, is punishable with imprisonment up to 6 years and fine, with continuing-offence provisions for ongoing violations.

8 · Significance / related developments

  • Supplemented later by the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Cess Act, 1977 (a cess on water consumption by polluting industries to fund Board activities — repealed in 2017 after GST was introduced).
  • Provided the scientific/legal backbone for later programmes like the Ganga Action Plan and Namami Gange.
  • The Water (Amendment) Bill, 2024 proposes to decriminalize minor offences and allow certain categories of plants to be exempted from statutory restrictions — a recent reform still under discussion.

Quick-recall skeleton

Act 6/1974, Art. 252 (12 states) → 8 Ch/64 Sec → objective: prevent pollution + restore wholesomeness → CPCB (Sec 16, advises Centre, coordinates states) + SPCB (Sec 17, advises state, grants consent) → consent mechanism (Sec 25–26, CTE/CTO) → penalty: 6 yrs + fine → Cess Act 1977 (repealed 2017, GST) → 2024 Amendment Bill

Topic 06 · 15 marks

Air (Prevention & Control of Pollution) Act, 1981

Nov 2024 · Dec 2022

Structure plan — same skeleton as the Water Act Background → Objectives → Structure → Boards & functions → Air Pollution Control Area + consent → Penalties → Relation to Water Act / 1987 amendment. Easy to compare in the exam.

1 · Background

  • Enacted to implement India’s commitment from the Stockholm Conference, 1972 on the Human Environment, specifically to tackle air pollution, which the Water Act, 1974 did not cover.
  • Came into force on 16 May 1981 (passed under the Union’s residuary/implementing-international-decisions power, similar basis as other post-Stockholm laws).
  • Preceded by the Water Act, 1974 and later followed/complemented by the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.

2 · Objectives

  • To prevent, control, and abate air pollution in India.
  • To maintain the quality of air necessary for sustaining human health, animals, plants, and property.
  • To establish Boards to carry out these functions and confer them with necessary powers.

3 · Structure of the Act

  • Contains 7 Chapters and 54 Sections.
  • Does not create new Boards — instead, it designates the existing CPCB and SPCBs (already constituted under the Water Act, 1974) to additionally function as air pollution regulators.
  • In states with no SPCB under the Water Act, a new Board is constituted under Section 5 of the Air Act.

4 · CPCB functions (central level)

  • Advises the Central Government on matters relating to improvement of air quality and prevention/control of air pollution.
  • Plans and executes nationwide programs for air pollution control.
  • Coordinates activities of SPCBs and resolves disputes among them.
  • Lays down and revises National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) — the key statutory link to the air standards topic above.

5 · SPCB functions (state level)

  • Executes CPCB’s air pollution programs at the state level.
  • Carries out inspections of air pollution control areas and industrial plants.
  • Advises the State Government on feasibility/location of industries from an air-pollution standpoint.
  • Lays down emission standards for industries within the state.

6 · Air Pollution Control Area + consent — core operational provisions

  • Section 19: the State Government can declare any area as an “Air Pollution Control Area”, after consultation with the SPCB.
  • Section 21: no person can establish or operate an industrial plant in such an area without prior consent of the SPCB — the key enforcement tool, structurally identical to the Water Act’s consent mechanism.
  • Section 22: no industry operating in a control area can emit air pollutants in excess of standards laid down by the SPCB.

7 · Penalties

  • Section 37: failure to obtain consent under Section 21, or violating consent conditions → imprisonment of 1.5 years to 6 years, with fine, and additional fine for continuing contravention.

8 · Relation to Water Act & later amendment

  • The Air Act deliberately reused the Water Act’s institutional machinery (CPCB/SPCB) instead of duplicating it — making the two Acts structurally parallel (both use consent mechanism, Board-based enforcement).
  • The original 1981 Act did not cover noise pollution; this was added later via the 1987 Amendment, which brought noise under the definition of “air pollutant.”

Quick-recall skeleton

16 May 1981, post-Stockholm 1972 → 7 Ch/54 Sec → reuses CPCB/SPCB from Water Act 1974 → CPCB: advises Centre, sets NAAQS | SPCB: executes, inspects, emission standards → Sec 19 (Air Pollution Control Area) → Sec 21 (prior consent) → Sec 22 (emission limit) → Sec 37 penalty (1.5–6 yrs + fine) → 1987 Amendment added noise
