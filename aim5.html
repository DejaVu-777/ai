Hydroelectric project — clearance procedure & impact assessment

Hydroelectric projects, despite being a renewable energy source, cause significant environmental and social disruption due to large-scale land submergence, deforestation, and population displacement. Hence in India, such projects (especially those >25 MW or with large dams) mandatorily require Environmental Clearance (EC) under the EIA Notification, 2006, following a structured multi-stage procedure before construction can begin.

Clearance procedure — four stages

01
ScreeningDetermines whether the project needs clearance at all, and whether it falls under Category A (cleared by MoEF&CC at central level) or Category B (cleared by SEIAA)
02
ScopingThe expert committee (EAC) decides the Terms of Reference (ToR) — what specific impacts (submergence, seismicity, biodiversity) the EIA study must cover
03
Public consultationPublic hearing at the project site to record concerns of local people / Project Affected Persons (PAPs), plus written responses from stakeholders
04
AppraisalThe EAC scrutinizes the final EIA report and EMP and recommends grant/rejection of EC to the regulatory authority

Key impact assessment parameters

  • Submergence of land — large forest and agricultural area lost permanently under the reservoir.
  • Displacement & resettlement — villages/settlements submerged, requiring a formal Rehabilitation & Resettlement (R&R) Plan.
  • Loss of biodiversity — destruction of terrestrial flora/fauna habitat and disruption of aquatic ecology (fish migration blocked by dam).
  • Downstream flow alteration — reduced river discharge affects irrigation, drinking water, and downstream ecosystems (e-flow norms now mandatory).
  • Seismic risk — reservoir-induced seismicity due to added water load on fault zones.
  • Sedimentation — trapping of silt behind the dam reduces reservoir life and starves downstream agricultural land of nutrients.
  • Greenhouse gas emissions — submerged vegetation decomposition releases methane, often overlooked despite hydro being labelled “clean”.
  • Cultural/religious site loss — temples, heritage sites, burial grounds submerged.

Mitigation measures

  • Compensatory afforestation for forest land diverted.
  • Fish ladders/passes to maintain aquatic connectivity.
  • Maintaining minimum environmental flow (e-flow) downstream.
  • Resettlement colonies with livelihood restoration for PAPs.

Topic 02 · 15 marks

Airport project — EIA need & impact identification

Airport projects are classified as Category A projects under the EIA Notification, 2006, requiring mandatory clearance from MoEF&CC due to their large land footprint, high traffic-induced pollution, and significant social displacement. Since airports are long-term, irreversible land-use changes located near urban/semi-urban areas, a detailed EIA is essential to balance connectivity & economic growth against environmental and community costs.

Why EIA is needed for airport projects

  • Large land requirement — runways, terminals, and buffer zones consume hundreds of hectares, often agricultural or forest land.
  • Population displacement — villages/settlements near the site require resettlement, similar to highway/dam projects.
  • Irreversibility — once built, land-use cannot easily revert; long operational lifespan locks in impacts for decades.
  • Multiple impact types — noise, air, water, and ecological impacts occur simultaneously, needing integrated assessment.
  • Statutory requirement — no Environmental Clearance (EC), no construction can legally begin.

Key impacts identified

  • Noise pollution — aircraft takeoff/landing causes severe noise exceeding permissible limits near residential zones; affects nearby schools/hospitals.
  • Air quality degradation — aircraft engine emissions + increased vehicular traffic around the airport raise local pollution levels.
  • Loss of agricultural/forest land — large-scale land acquisition reduces farming livelihoods and green cover.
  • Hydrological changes — paving large areas increases surface runoff, reduces groundwater recharge, and can cause local flooding.
  • Bird hazard & biodiversity loss — airports often disturb wetlands/bird habitats; conversely, bird presence creates a bird-strike safety risk to aircraft.
  • Displacement & R&R issues — compensation and rehabilitation of Project Affected Persons (PAPs).
  • Construction-phase impacts — dust, noise, and soil erosion during the building phase.
  • Socio-economic impact — positive: boosts tourism, trade, employment; negative: land value disputes, livelihood loss for displaced farmers.

Mitigation measures

  • Noise barriers and restricted night-flight schedules near residential areas.
  • Proper stormwater drainage design to compensate for loss of natural permeable surface.
  • Green belt/buffer plantation around the perimeter to control dust and noise.
  • R&R package with livelihood restoration for displaced families.

Quarry / mining project — clearance procedure & impact assessment

Quarrying and mining projects involve extraction of minerals, stone, or sand from the earth’s surface, causing direct and often irreversible damage to land, water, and ecology. Under the EIA Notification, 2006, mining projects (based on lease area) fall under Category A (≥50 ha) or Category B (<50 ha), requiring Environmental Clearance (EC) before operations can begin, along with separate Mining Plan/Lease approval from the Department of Mines.

Clearance procedure — four stages

01
ScreeningClassifies the project as Category A (cleared by MoEF&CC) or Category B (cleared by SEIAA) based on lease area and mineral type
02
ScopingExpert Appraisal Committee (EAC) finalizes the Terms of Reference (ToR), specifying which impacts — dust, blasting, groundwater — must be studied in detail
03
Public consultationMandatory public hearing near the mine site to record objections/suggestions from local residents and affected communities
04
AppraisalEAC reviews the final EIA report + EMP and recommends grant or rejection of clearance

Key impact assessment parameters

  • Land degradation — permanent alteration of topography, creation of open pits and overburden dumps.
  • Loss of topsoil & vegetation — removal of the fertile soil layer makes post-mining land unfit for agriculture without reclamation.
  • Air pollution — dust emission from blasting, excavation, crushing, and transport of material.
  • Noise & vibration — from blasting operations and heavy machinery, affecting nearby settlements and wildlife.
  • Groundwater depletion/contamination — deep excavation can intersect aquifers, lowering the water table or polluting it with mine effluent.
  • Water body siltation — runoff carrying loose debris increases sedimentation in nearby rivers/streams.
  • Habitat destruction — disturbance/loss of flora and fauna in and around the mining lease area.
  • Slope instability — improper excavation can trigger landslides or slope failure, especially in hilly terrain.
  • Occupational health hazards — workers exposed to silica dust (risk of silicosis), noise, and accident-prone conditions.

Mitigation measures

  • Progressive mine closure & reclamation — backfilling pits, re-vegetation with native species.
  • Water sprinkling on haul roads and crushers to suppress dust.
  • Controlled/delayed blasting techniques to limit noise and vibration.
  • Garland drains and siltation ponds to prevent sediment entering water bodies.
  • Providing PPE (masks, ear protection) and regular health check-ups for workers.

Highway project — impact assessment, with case study

Case study — Nagpur–Mumbai Samruddhi Mahamarg, Package-III

Highway projects, though essential for connectivity, trade, and regional development, involve large-scale linear land acquisition cutting across forests, farmland, and settlements. A relevant case study is the Nagpur–Mumbai Samruddhi Mahamarg (Package-III: Jalna to Aurangabad, 155 km), where land was acquired through land pooling instead of traditional acquisition, and a detailed EIA was conducted to assess environmental and social consequences before construction.

Case study overview

  • Project: 701.128 km expressway across 10 districts from Vidarbha through Marathwada to Konkan, implemented by MSRDC.
  • Land pooling model — farmers transfer land ownership to the government agency, which develops infrastructure (differs from traditional land acquisition).
  • Total land requirement of about 10,000 ha, with Package-III alone requiring 26.877 ha forest land and 1858 ha non-forest/agricultural land.

Key impacts identified

  • Deforestation — felling of 5,309 trees (399 forest + 4,910 non-forest), causing loss of habitat for avian fauna.
  • Loss of agricultural land — diversion of 1,858 ha affects farmer livelihoods and food security.
  • Impact on water bodies — disturbance to 3 rivers, 26 ponds, 3 canals, and 59 local streams; risk of sediment deposition and turbidity.
  • Groundwater disturbance — potential impact on 356 wells along the corridor.
  • Impact on cultural/religious sites — 7 religious/cultural properties affected by the alignment.
  • Air & dust pollution — from earthwork, borrow areas (2.8 Cr. cum of borrow earth), and vehicular movement during construction.
  • Noise pollution — from construction machinery and post-completion vehicular traffic.
  • Displacement & resettlement — demolition of structures within the Right of Way (ROW), requiring R&R measures.
  • Occupational health & safety risks — to construction workers from dust, noise, and site hazards.
  • Positive impacts — reduced travel time, lower vehicle operating costs, 50% reduction in accidents, better connectivity boosting regional development.

Mitigation measures

  • Compensatory afforestation in degraded forest areas + avenue plantation along the corridor.
  • Sediment traps and proper drainage to protect water bodies from construction runoff.
  • Rehabilitation Action Plan (RAP) ensuring equitable compensation for Project Affected Persons (PAPs).
  • Restriction on tree felling beyond the toe line, with replanting commitments.
  • Low-noise equipment and scheduling construction activity to daytime near residential zones.

Solid waste management project — impact assessment

Solid Waste Management (SWM) projects — including landfills, waste-to-energy plants, and composting facilities — are essential to handle growing municipal waste but can themselves become significant pollution sources if poorly planned. Under the EIA Notification, 2006, common/municipal solid waste management facilities handling large volumes require Environmental Clearance, with site selection and impact assessment being critical since these projects operate near or within urban/semi-urban areas for decades.

Why EIA is needed

  • Long operational life — landfills remain active (and leach pollutants) for decades, making impacts long-term and difficult to reverse.
  • Proximity to habitation — site often located near residential areas, raising health and nuisance concerns.
  • Multiple pollution pathways — air, water, soil, and groundwater can all be affected simultaneously.
  • Mandatory under law before siting, construction, and operation of landfill/treatment facility.

Key impacts identified

  • Leachate generation — percolation of rainwater through waste produces toxic leachate, contaminating soil and groundwater if not lined/contained.
  • Groundwater & surface water pollution — untreated leachate seeping into aquifers or nearby streams/ponds.
  • Air pollution — methane and landfill gas emissions from decomposing organic waste (also a major greenhouse gas contributor).
  • Foul odour — decomposition causes persistent odour nuisance affecting nearby residents.
  • Soil contamination — heavy metals and toxic substances from waste leaching into surrounding soil.
  • Vector-borne disease risk — open dumping attracts rats, flies, and stray animals, spreading disease.
  • Fire hazard — methane build-up can cause spontaneous landfill fires, releasing toxic smoke.
  • Loss of land value & aesthetics — visual pollution and reduced property value near dump sites.
  • Traffic & noise — movement of heavy waste-collection vehicles causes congestion and noise in surrounding areas.
  • Occupational health hazards — waste-pickers/workers exposed to toxic fumes, sharp objects, and biological hazards.

Mitigation measures

  • Scientific sanitary landfill design with impermeable liners and leachate collection/treatment system.
  • Gas collection systems to capture methane (can be used for energy recovery).
  • Segregation at source (wet/dry/biomedical) to reduce landfill load and enable composting/recycling.
  • Buffer green belt around the site to control odour and dust dispersion.
  • Regular groundwater monitoring in and around the facility.
  • PPE and health check-ups for sanitation workers.
